A Beginner’s Guide · arXiv 2006.11239

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

How three researchers taught a computer to paint pictures by first teaching it to clean up static — explained from zero.

Jonathan Ho · Ajay Jain · Pieter Abbeel UC Berkeley NeurIPS 2020

TWELVE SHORT CHAPTERS · ~35 MIN READ · ORIGINAL PDF

The whole paper in one sentence

If you slowly bury a picture under random static, and train a neural network to undo one tiny step of that burying, you can start from pure static and “un-bury” your way to a brand-new, realistic picture.

Chapter 1

The Big Question

“What I cannot create, I do not understand.”— Richard Feynman

Imagine you have looked at ten thousand photos of cats. Could you now draw a new cat — one that is not a copy of any photo, but still clearly a cat? Humans can. This paper is about teaching a computer to do the same thing. Programs that do this are called generative models: they generate new examples that look like they came from the same collection as their training data.

A digital image is just a grid of numbers. A small 32 × 32 colour picture has 32 × 32 × 3 = 3,072 numbers (red, green and blue for every pixel). Picking 3,072 numbers at random gives you TV static. Almost every possible grid of numbers is static — real-looking images are an unbelievably tiny island in an ocean of noise. A generative model has to learn where that island is.

What existed before (2020)

Researchers already had several families of generative models. You don’t need to know how they work — only that each had a catch:

FamilyThe idea in a phraseThe catch
GANsA forger and a detective train against each other.Sharp images, but training is famously unstable.
VAEsSqueeze an image into a short code, then expand it back.Stable, but images tend to look blurry.
AutoregressiveWrite the image one pixel at a time, like text.Very slow to generate; fixed pixel order.
FlowsA reversible mathematical funnel from noise to images.Strict design rules limit the network.

Diffusion models had been proposed in 2015 by Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues, inspired by physics. They were stable and elegant — but nobody had made them produce good-looking images. This paper’s headline claim: they can, and they can beat the best GANs on some benchmarks.

Chapter 2

The Core Idea

Drop ink into a glass of water. Over a minute it spreads out until the water is evenly tinted. That spreading is called diffusion, and it only runs one way: you never see tinted water spontaneously gather back into a neat drop. Running it backwards seems impossible.

But here is the trick. If you film the ink and look at two neighbouring frames, the change between them is tiny. Undoing one tiny change is easy to learn. Undo a thousand tiny changes in a row and you’ve reversed the whole thing.

The paper does exactly this with pictures and random static:

  1. Forward process (destroy): take a real image and add a little random noise. Repeat 1,000 times. The image fades into pure static. This step needs no learning — it is a fixed recipe.
  2. Reverse process (create): train a neural network to remove one step’s worth of noise. Then start from fresh random static and apply it 1,000 times. A new image emerges.

Uses the paper’s exact noise schedule. “Replay backwards” cheats — it already knows the original picture. The trained network in this paper has to guess it.

Chapter 3

Toolkit for Beginners

Five ideas are enough to read the rest of this guide. Skip any you already know.

Idea 1

Gaussian noise

Random numbers that follow the “bell curve”: most are near zero, a few are larger. Written 𝒩(0, I) — mean 0, spread 1, every pixel independent. It’s the static on an old TV.

Idea 2

Neural network

A big adjustable function. You feed it numbers (a noisy image) and it outputs numbers (its guess). Its millions of internal knobs are called weights, written θ (theta).

Idea 3

Loss & training

A loss is a score of how wrong the guess was. Training = repeatedly nudge the knobs in the direction that makes the loss smaller (gradient descent).

Idea 4

Markov chain

A sequence of steps where each step only depends on the one just before. x₀ → x₁ → x₂ … like footprints: where you step next depends only on where you stand now.

Idea 5

Probability distribution

A description of which outcomes are likely. “The distribution of cat photos” means: which grids of numbers look like cats. Written q(x) for real data, pθ(x) for the model’s version.

Notation

x₀ … xT

x₀ is the clean image. xt is it after t noise steps. xT (T = 1000) is pure noise. Same size at every step.

Chapter 4

The Forward Process: Adding Noise

Each forward step does two things: it shrinks the current image a tiny bit and adds a tiny bit of fresh noise. How much noise at step t is set by a number βt (“beta”), called the noise schedule.

\[ q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t \mathbf{I}\right) \]
Read it aloud

“The next image xt is the previous image scaled by √(1−βt) — slightly faded — plus random noise of strength βt.” The shrinking keeps the numbers from growing forever, so the end result is noise of a standard size.

The paper chooses T = 1000 steps and lets β grow in a straight line from 0.0001 at step 1 to 0.02 at step 1000: gentle at first, harsher later. These numbers are fixed — nothing here is learned.

The shortcut that makes training fast

To get x500, must you add noise 500 times? No. Adding several bell-curve noises together gives another bell-curve noise, so all the steps collapse into one formula. Define αt = 1 − βt and ᾱt (“alpha-bar”) = the product of all α’s up to t. Then:

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\;x_0 \;+\; \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}) \]
Read it aloud

“A noisy image at any step is just a blend: some amount of the original picture plus some amount of one random noise image ε (epsilon).” The two dials are exactly what the slider in Chapter 2 shows.

Adding Noise4
Chapter 5

The Reverse Process: Removing Noise

Now the hard direction. Given a noisy image xt, what did the slightly-less-noisy xt−1 look like? Because each forward step was tiny, the answer is also “a bell curve around some best guess.” The network’s job is to supply that best guess.

The paper’s key design choice: predict the noise

There were three obvious options for what the network could output:

  1. The clean image x₀ directly — the authors found this gave worse images.
  2. The average of the slightly-cleaner image (called μ̃, “mu-tilde”) — the standard choice.
  3. The noise ε that was mixed in — the paper’s choice.

Why does option 3 help? Remember the blend formula: if you know the noisy image and you know which noise was added, you can subtract it out. So “guess the noise” is the same information as “guess the image” — but it turns out to be a much friendlier target for the network to learn. We write the network as εθ(xt, t): “given this noisy image and the step number, what noise do you think is in it?”

Analogy

A restorer cleaning an old painting doesn’t repaint it from memory. They identify the grime and lift it off. The network learns to recognise grime.

The network itself

  • U-Net: a network shaped like a “U” — it shrinks the image to understand the big picture, then expands it back, with shortcut connections passing fine detail across. Its output is the same size as its input, perfect for “here is the noise in every pixel.”
  • One network for all 1,000 steps: it is told which step it’s on using a sinusoidal time embedding (the same trick Transformers use to encode word position), because removing heavy noise and light noise are different jobs.
  • Self-attention at the 16 × 16 resolution lets distant parts of the image coordinate (e.g. both eyes on a face match).
  • Size: 35.7 million weights for small images (CIFAR-10), 114 million for 256 × 256 images.

Chapter 6

Training: A Guessing Game

Training is astonishingly simple — this simplicity is a big part of why the paper mattered. It is a game played millions of times: hide some known noise in a real image, ask the network to find it, score its answer.

The score being minimised is called Lsimple:

\[ L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_0,\,\epsilon}\Big[\, \big\| \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\ t\big) \big\|^2 \Big] \]
Read it aloud

“On average over random images, random steps and random noise: square the difference between the real noise and the network’s guess.” 𝔼 means “average over”. ‖·‖² means “add up the squared differences in every pixel”. It’s the plain mean-squared-error you’d use for any prediction task.

In code, the whole training step is a handful of lines:

for x0 in data:                         # a batch of real images, scaled to [-1, 1]
    t   = randint(1, 1000)              # random step
    eps = randn_like(x0)                # random noise
    xt  = sqrt(abar[t]) * x0 + sqrt(1 - abar[t]) * eps
    loss = ((eps - model(xt, t)) ** 2).mean()
    loss.backward(); optimizer.step()   # nudge the weights
Real numbers from the paper

The CIFAR-10 model trained for 800,000 steps with batches of 128 images, taking 10.6 hours on a Google TPU v3-8 (roughly 8 high-end GPUs).

Chapter 7

Making New Images (Sampling)

Once trained, generating an image — called sampling — means starting with pure random static and asking the network to clean it, one step at a time, a thousand times.

\[ x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \Big) + \sigma_t z \]
Read it aloud

“Take the current image, subtract the right-sized slice of the predicted noise, re-brighten it slightly (divide by √αt), then add a small fresh random kick σtz.” The kick keeps results varied and stops the process from getting stuck; it’s skipped on the very last step. The paper sets σt² = βt — a fixed value, not learned.

What you’d see while it runs

The paper (Figure 6) shows the network’s running guess of the final image during sampling. Big shapes appear first — blobs of colour, layout, the outline of a face — and fine details come last, like a painter blocking in shapes before adding eyelashes.

The cost

1,000 network calls per image makes sampling slow: 17 seconds for a batch of 256 small images, but 300 seconds for 128 images at 256 × 256. A GAN needs just one call.

Chapter 8

Why It Works (The Theory, Gently)

You can use diffusion models without this chapter. But this is where the paper’s scientific contribution lives, so here is the story without the heavy algebra.

8.1 The “honest” score: the variational bound

The principled goal of a generative model is to give high probability to real images. That probability is impossible to compute directly here, so — like VAEs — the paper uses a variational bound (also called the ELBO): a quantity that is always worse than or equal to the true score, so improving it pushes the true score up too.

For diffusion models, this bound neatly breaks into one piece per step: “how well does the network’s guess of xt−1 match the true answer?” Because both are bell curves, each piece has an exact formula — no extra randomness needed.

8.2 The simplification that made images good

When the authors worked out that bound with the “predict the noise” design, each step’s piece became mean-squared-error on the noise, multiplied by a step-dependent weight. Their bold move: throw the weights away. That gives Lsimple from Chapter 6.

Dropping the weights means the network spends less effort on the easy, nearly-clean steps and more on the hard, very-noisy ones. The result is worse on the formal probability score but much better-looking images. (Chapter 9 shows the numbers.)

8.3 A surprising connection

A separate line of research (Song & Ermon, 2019) generated images using score matching — learning which direction makes an image “more realistic” at many noise levels — and Langevin dynamics, a physics-inspired method that walks downhill toward realism with small random jiggles.

The paper’s main theoretical result

With the “predict the noise” design, training a diffusion model is mathematically equivalent to that score-matching approach, and the sampling loop looks just like Langevin dynamics. Two research communities were secretly working on the same thing. Predicting the noise is (up to scaling) predicting the direction toward realistic images.

Chapter 9

Results

How do you grade “realistic”? The paper uses two standard scores, both computed by a pretrained image-recognition network:

  • FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) — how far the statistics of generated images are from real ones. Lower is better.
  • IS (Inception Score) — are images clear and varied? Higher is better.
  • NLL (bits per dimension) — the formal probability score. Lower is better.

Chapter 10

Bonus Discoveries

Great images, mediocre probability scores

The NLL of ~3.7 bits per dimension is worse than top autoregressive models (Sparse Transformer: 2.80). The authors dug into why: more than half of those bits go into describing details too small for a human to see. The model spends its “attention” on what matters visually — which makes it an excellent lossy compressor (like JPEG), even if not the best lossless one.

Progressive decoding

Because big shapes appear before small details, you could “send” an image by transmitting the denoising steps in order: the receiver sees a rough version early and sharper versions as more arrives — like a slow-loading web image. The paper shows this is a generalised form of autoregressive (one-piece-at-a-time) generation, but where the “pieces” are levels of detail instead of individual pixels.

Interpolation

Take two face photos, add noise to both (step 500), blend the noisy versions, then denoise. The output smoothly varies pose, skin tone, hairstyle, expression and background between the two people. Add more noise and the blends become coarser and more inventive.

Chapter 11

Limits & Legacy

What the paper doesn’t solve

  • Slow sampling — 1,000 network calls per image.
  • Probability scores lag behind the best likelihood models.
  • No control — it makes a random image from the training distribution; you can’t yet say “draw a red bicycle.”

Why it became one of the most important AI papers

This paper showed that a simple, stable recipe — add noise, learn to predict it — could match the best GANs. The research world quickly built on it:

  • DDIM (Song, Meng & Ermon, 2020) — same trained network, sampling in tens of steps instead of 1,000.
  • Improved DDPM (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021) — learned variances done stably, better schedules.
  • “Diffusion Models Beat GANs” (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) — guidance to steer generation toward a chosen class.
  • Latent Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) — run diffusion on a compressed version of the image; the basis of Stable Diffusion.
  • Text-to-image systems such as DALL·E 2 and Imagen, and later video and audio generators, all use diffusion at their core.

The ε-prediction and Lsimple loss introduced here remain the default starting point taught today.

“Since diffusion models seem to have excellent inductive biases for image data, we look forward to investigating their utility in other data modalities.”— Ho, Jain & Abbeel, Conclusion

Chapter 12

Recap & Quiz

Six-line recap

  1. Destroying an image with noise is easy; creating one is hard. Do the hard part in 1,000 tiny steps.
  2. Forward: a fixed recipe blends image and noise; any step can be reached in one jump.
  3. Reverse: one U-Net, told the step number, predicts the noise hidden in an image.
  4. Training: plain mean-squared-error between real and predicted noise (Lsimple).
  5. Sampling: start from static, subtract predicted noise 1,000 times.
  6. Result: FID 3.17 on CIFAR-10, state-of-the-art in 2020 — and the foundation of modern image generators.

Check yourself

Does the forward (noising) process need to be learned?
No. It is a fixed recipe with a pre-chosen schedule β₁ = 0.0001 to β₁₀₀₀ = 0.02. Only the reverse process has learnable weights.
What exactly does the neural network output?
Its guess of the noise ε that was mixed into the input image xt, given the step number t.
Why can training jump straight to step 700 without running 700 steps?
Because stacked Gaussian noises combine into one Gaussian, so xt = √ᾱt·x₀ + √(1−ᾱt)·ε gives any step directly.
Why does Lsimple give better images than the “correct” bound?
It drops the per-step weights, which down-weights the easy low-noise steps so the network focuses on the harder high-noise denoising that shapes the overall image.
What is the main practical downside?
Slow generation — 1,000 network evaluations per image. Later work like DDIM reduced this dramatically.
