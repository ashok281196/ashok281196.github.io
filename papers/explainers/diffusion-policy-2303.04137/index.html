Diffusion Policy

Visuomotor policy learning via action diffusion: how the trick that paints pictures from noise became the way robots learn skills from human demonstrations.

Cheng Chi* · Zhenjia Xu* · Siyuan Feng · Eric Cousineau · Yilun Du · Benjamin Burchfiel · Russ Tedrake · Shuran Song Columbia · Toyota Research Institute · MIT RSS 2023 · extended version 2024

FIFTEEN CHAPTERS · ~55 MIN READ · ORIGINAL PDF · PREREQUISITE (OPTIONAL): DDPM EXPLAINER

The whole paper in one sentence

Instead of training a robot’s brain to output one answer for “what should I do next?”, train it like an image-diffusion model to start from random noise and gradually refine a whole short plan of future movements. That lets it learn from messy, inconsistent human demonstrations and still move smoothly, decisively and robustly.

Chapter 1

Teaching a Robot by Showing It

“Tell me and I forget, teach me and I may remember, involve me and I learn.”Proverb

Programming a robot to fold a shirt line by line is nearly impossible: cloth bends in endless ways, and every rule you write breaks on the next shirt. An easier route is to show the robot. A person drives the robot through the task many times (with a joystick, a VR controller, or a device that mirrors their hand) while cameras and sensors record everything. Then a neural network learns to imitate.

This approach is called imitation learning, and its simplest form is behavior cloning: treat the recordings as a giant table of “when the robot saw this, the human did that,” and train a network to reproduce it.

The vocabulary you need

Observation · o

What the robot senses

Camera images (from fixed cameras and a camera on the wrist) plus proprioception, the robot’s own sense of where its arm and gripper are.

Action · a

What the robot does

A command for the next moment, e.g. “move the gripper to this 3D position and rotation, open it this wide.” Actions are lists of numbers: 2 for a flat pusher, 10 for one arm, 20 for two arms.

Policy · π

The robot’s brain

A function from observation to action: π(o) → a. Training a policy is the whole goal. A visuomotor policy goes straight from pixels (“visuo”) to motor commands.

Demonstration

One recorded attempt

A sequence of (observation, action) pairs from a human-driven run. The paper’s tasks use from about 100 to several hundred demos each.

Behavior cloning is just supervised learning, which makes it attractive. But as the next chapter shows, “copy the human” hides several traps, and each one is a place where earlier methods broke and Diffusion Policy does not.

Chapter 2

Why Copying Humans Is Hard

Trap 1: there is more than one right answer

Imagine a block sits between the robot and its goal. Sometimes the demonstrator went around the left side; sometimes around the right. Both are perfect. In maths-speak the distribution of correct actions is multimodal: it has more than one peak (“mode”).

A standard network trained with squared error learns the average of what it saw. The average of “go left” and “go right” is “go straight”, which means straight into the block. Other methods avoid averaging but pick a new side every instant, so the robot dithers. Try it:

Press run.

A toy, not the paper’s code: dashed lines are the two demonstrated routes. “One step at a time” samples a side independently at each step (like single-step mixture or clustering policies); “Diffusion Policy” samples a whole 16-step plan, executes 8 steps, then replans.

Run each a few times. The averaging policy crashes every time. The one-step sampler zig-zags and usually crashes. The chunked planner picks a side (sometimes left, sometimes right) and commits. That is the behaviour the paper shows on its real Push-T task (paper Figure 3).

Trap 2: high-dimensional outputs

Predicting one action at a time is jittery. Predicting a whole sequence (say 16 future actions × 10 numbers = 160 numbers) is smoother, but most methods that can represent multiple modes get much harder to train as the output grows.

Trap 3: unstable training

One family (energy-based “implicit” policies, Chapter 3) handles multiple modes but needs “negative samples” during training, which makes training jumpy: the score you care about rises and falls unpredictably, so you must test many saved checkpoints on the real robot to find a good one.

Trap 4: pauses, delays and surprises

  • Idle actions: humans pause (e.g. waiting for sauce to fill a ladle). Single-step policies learn to “pause forever” and get stuck.
  • Latency: cameras, network inference and robot communication add delay; the world moves on before the action arrives.
  • Perturbations: someone bumps the object, and the robot must re-plan, not blindly replay.
The paper’s promise

One method (diffusion over action sequences, run in a closed loop) that handles all four traps, with hyperparameters that barely need tuning between tasks.

Chapter 3

Three Ways to Write a Policy

The paper’s first figure sorts robot policies into three families by how the network turns an observation into an action.

(a) Explicit

Output the action directly

One forward pass: image in, action out.

  • Regression: one number per dimension (averages modes).
  • Mixture of Gaussians (LSTM-GMM / BC-RNN): a few bell curves; must guess how many.
  • Categorical / clustering (BET): bins + offsets; bins explode in high dimensions.
fast, but fragile with many modes
(b) Implicit

Score every action, pick the best

Learn an “energy” E(o, a): low for good actions. Acting = search for the lowest-energy action (IBC).

  • Naturally multimodal: many valleys allowed.
  • Training needs negative samples to estimate an intractable constant Z.
  • Unstable; checkpoint picking is painful.
expressive, but unstable
(c) Diffusion

Refine noise into an action

Start from random actions; repeatedly follow a learned “which way is better” arrow field.

  • Multimodal like (b), without needing Z.
  • Scales to long action sequences, like image pixels.
  • Training is ordinary mean-squared error, which is stable.
expressive and stable

The intuition is this. Picture all possible actions as a landscape. Explicit policies point at one spot. Implicit policies draw the whole landscape (energy) and then search it. Diffusion Policy learns only the slope of that landscape (its gradient), and slopes are far easier and steadier to learn than the landscape itself. Chapter 10 makes this precise.

Chapter 4

Diffusion Models in Two Minutes

Diffusion Policy is built directly on DDPM (Ho, Jain & Abbeel, 2020). If you’ve read our DDPM explainer you can skim this chapter. The short version:

  1. Destroy: take real data, add a little random noise, repeat K times until it is pure noise.
  2. Learn: train a network εθ to look at noisy data and guess the noise that was added.
  3. Create: start from pure noise and repeatedly subtract the network’s noise guess. Real-looking data appears.

The paper writes one cleaning step like this (k counts down from K to 0):

\[ x^{k-1} = \alpha\Big(x^{k} - \gamma\,\epsilon_\theta(x^{k}, k) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})\Big) \]
Read it aloud

“Take the current noisy sample, subtract γ times the predicted noise, add a little fresh randomness, and scale by α (slightly less than 1).” The three numbers α, γ, σ change with k. That is the noise schedule.

The paper then points out a useful connection: that step is the same shape as one step of gradient descent, the most common optimisation method in machine learning:

\[ x' = x - \gamma\,\nabla E(x) \]

So the noise-prediction network is secretly predicting a gradient field ∇E(x): at every point it gives an arrow pointing “downhill” toward realistic data. Sampling is noisy gradient descent along those arrows, a procedure called Stochastic Langevin Dynamics. The noise schedule plays the role of a learning-rate schedule.

Training is the same simple recipe as DDPM: take clean data x⁰, pick a random k, add noise εk of the matching size, and ask the network to recover it:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathrm{MSE}\big(\epsilon^{k},\; \epsilon_\theta(x^{0} + \epsilon^{k},\, k)\big) \]
Diffusion in 2 Minutes4
Chapter 5

The Big Idea: Diffuse Actions, Not Pixels

DDPM generates images. Diffusion Policy changes two things so it can generate robot behaviour instead.

Change 1: the output is a sequence of actions

Instead of x being an image, it becomes At: a short movie of future actions, e.g. the next 16 gripper poses. To a diffusion model this is just a small “image” 16 steps long and a few numbers wide, which is easy compared with a 256 × 256 photo.

Change 2: the denoising is conditioned on what the robot sees

The network also receives the recent observations Ot as context. Every cleaning step now reads:

\[ A_t^{k-1} = \alpha\Big(A_t^{k} - \gamma\,\epsilon_\theta(O_t,\, A_t^{k},\, k) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})\Big) \]

and training becomes:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathrm{MSE}\big(\epsilon^{k},\; \epsilon_\theta(O_t,\; A_t^{0} + \epsilon^{k},\; k)\big) \]
Why “conditioned on” matters

An earlier robotics diffusion model (Diffuser, Janner et al. 2022) generated observations and actions together, the joint distribution p(A, O). That forces the network to imagine future camera images, which is slow and error-prone. Diffusion Policy only learns p(At | Ot): “given what I see, what should I do?” The image is processed once per decision, not once per denoising step. That is a big speed-up, and it also makes training the vision encoder end-to-end practical.

Chapter 6

Horizons & Receding-Horizon Control

A good plan must be smooth (no jitter) and responsive (react if the world changes). Diffusion Policy balances these with three numbers:

SymbolNameMeaningTypical
ToObservation horizonHow many recent observations the policy looks at.2
TpPrediction horizonHow many future actions it plans.16
TaAction (execution) horizonHow many of those it actually executes before re-planning.8

This is a classic idea from control engineering called receding-horizon control (or model-predictive control): always plan a little further ahead than you act, then re-plan. Diffusion Policy can also warm-start the next plan from the unexecuted tail of the previous one, for extra smoothness.

The trade-off, measured

  • Ta too small (re-plan every step): actions can flip between modes → jitter (Chapter 2 demo), and the policy overfits to idle pauses.
  • Ta too large: the robot is slow to react to surprises.
  • The ablation (paper Figure 5) found Ta = 8 best on most tasks, and performance held at its peak with up to 4 steps of simulated latency.
  • State-based policies didn’t care much about To; vision policies (especially the CNN version) got worse with longer history, so To = 2 is the default.

Chapter 7

Inside the Network

There are two parts: a vision encoder that turns camera images into a compact summary Ot, and a noise-prediction network εθ that does the denoising. The paper offers two designs for the second part.

7.1 The vision encoder

  • A standard ResNet-18, trained from scratch together with the policy (“end-to-end”), with no ImageNet pretraining required.
  • Spatial softmax pooling instead of global average pooling, so the summary keeps where things are in the image (crucial for manipulation).
  • GroupNorm instead of BatchNorm, because BatchNorm misbehaves with the weight-averaging (EMA) used by diffusion models.
  • Each camera view gets its own encoder; each time step is encoded separately and the results are concatenated into Ot.

7.2 Option A: CNN-based Diffusion Policy (the default)

A 1D temporal U-Net, adapted from Diffuser: it convolves along time over the 16-step action sequence. The observation summary and the step number k are injected into every layer with FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation): the observation decides, channel by channel, how much to scale and shift the network’s internal features. Think of the camera view turning a mixing desk’s knobs.

Works out of the box on most tasks. Weak spot: convolutions prefer smooth, low-frequency signals, so it over-smooths actions that must change sharply (e.g. velocity commands).

7.3 Option B: Time-series Diffusion Transformer

A transformer (from minGPT) where each noisy action is a token, with the step k as an extra first token. Observations enter through cross-attention in every block, and a causal mask lets each action attend only to itself and earlier actions. Each output token predicts the noise for its action.

Best on the hardest, fastest-changing state-based tasks, but more sensitive to hyperparameters.

The authors’ recommendation

Start with the CNN version on a new task. Switch to the transformer only if the task is complex or needs rapid action changes, and budget time for tuning.

7.4 Pretrained vision: does it help?

On the simulated Square task (CNN policy, 500 epochs), success rates were:

Chapter 9

Making It Fast Enough for a Robot

An image generator can take seconds. A robot can’t: it needs a fresh plan many times a second. Four design choices make that possible.

  1. Observation as condition, not output. The vision encoder runs once per decision; only the small action chunk is denoised repeatedly.
  2. DDIM sampling. DDIM (Song et al., 2021) lets a model trained with 100 noise steps sample with far fewer. The real-world setup used 100 training / ~10 inference iterations → 0.1 s latency on an Nvidia RTX 3080.
  3. Action chunks hide the delay. While the robot executes 8 actions, the next plan can be computed; position-control plans stay valid even if they arrive a few steps late.
  4. Squared-cosine noise schedule (from Improved DDPM, Nichol & Dhariwal 2021) worked best empirically for control signals.
Position vs. velocity control

An action can mean “go to this position” or “move at this velocity.” Most earlier behavior-cloning work used velocity. Diffusion Policy works better with position control (paper Figure 4), while the baselines got worse when switched to position. Two reasons: position commands make the multiple-modes problem sharper (which diffusion handles and others don’t), and position plans don’t accumulate error the way integrated velocities do, which suits executing chunks.

Chapter 10

Why It Works So Well

10.1 Multiple modes

Where does the “choose left or right” behaviour come from? Two sources of randomness: each plan starts from a different random noise sample, which lands in a different “valley” (mode) of the action landscape; and the small random kicks during denoising let samples settle cleanly into one valley. Because the whole 16-step chunk is denoised together, every action in the chunk agrees on the same valley. That is the commitment you saw in the Chapter 2 demo.

10.2 Sequences fix jitter and pauses

  • Temporal consistency: methods that sample each step independently (BC-RNN, BET) can alternate between valid routes and jitter.
  • Idle-action robustness: a chunk that includes a pause and what comes after it doesn’t get stuck in the pause. Single-step BC-RNN and IBC often froze on the real robot when pauses weren’t filtered out.

10.3 Stable training: the key equation

An implicit policy (IBC) defines action probabilities through an energy Eθ:

\[ p_\theta(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{o}) = \frac{e^{-E_\theta(\mathbf{o},\mathbf{a})}}{Z(\mathbf{o},\theta)} \]

The denominator Z adds up e−E over every possible action. That is impossible to compute exactly, so IBC estimates it with a handful of random “negative” actions. That estimate is noisy, which makes training noisy: in the paper’s Figure 6, IBC’s training loss falls smoothly while its actual success rate jumps up and down.

Diffusion Policy learns the score, the gradient of log-probability with respect to the action:

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf{a}} \log p(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{o}) = -\nabla_{\mathbf{a}} E_\theta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{o}) - \underbrace{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}} \log Z(\mathbf{o},\theta)}_{=\,0} \;\approx\; -\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{o}) \]
Read it aloud

“Z depends only on the observation, not on the action, so its slope with respect to the action is zero, and it disappears.” The noise-prediction network approximates the score directly, so neither training nor sampling ever needs Z. Same expressive power as an energy model, none of its instability.

Chapter 11

A Control-Theory Sanity Check

The extended paper adds a neat check: what does Diffusion Policy learn in the simplest possible case? Take a linear system, where the next state is a linear function of the current state and action, plus random disturbance:

\[ \mathbf{s}_{t+1} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_t + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{a}_t + \mathbf{w}_t, \qquad \mathbf{w}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_w) \]

and suppose the demonstrator is a classic linear feedback controller at = −K st (for example the textbook LQR controller). Then:

  • With a 1-step plan, the optimal denoiser recovers exactly a = −Ks, and DDIM sampling converges to it. Sanity: passed.
  • With a longer plan, predicting action t+t′ requires at+t′ = −K(A − BK)t′st. In other words, to clone a state-dependent behaviour over a horizon, the policy must implicitly learn a model of the system’s dynamics (A and B).
  • If the system or the controller is nonlinear, future actions can become multimodal again, which is where diffusion is needed.

Long-horizon multimodality

On tasks where the order of sub-goals varies, the gains are largest: +32% on Block Pushing’s p2 metric (both blocks pushed in) and +213% on Kitchen’s p4 metric (4 objects interacted with).

Ablations worth remembering

  • Action horizon: 8 steps works best (Chapter 6).
  • Latency: no loss in performance with up to 4 steps of delay; velocity control suffers more.
  • Training stability: the best hyperparameters were mostly the same across tasks; IBC needed checkpoint hunting.

Limits & Legacy

Limitations the authors list

  • It is still behavior cloning: it can only be as good as the demonstrations, and needs enough of them. It doesn’t learn from failures or rewards (though diffusion policies can be combined with reinforcement learning).
  • Compute and latency: heavier than an LSTM-GMM. Action chunks hide much of it, but very high-rate control may still need faster samplers, e.g. consistency models or better ODE solvers.

Why it mattered

Diffusion Policy turned “diffusion for robots” from an idea into a default. Its recipe (predict action chunks, condition on observations, run receding-horizon, use position control) became a standard starting point for imitation learning. Later systems built on it or its ideas, for example:

  • UMI (Universal Manipulation Interface, Chi et al. 2024): hand-held grippers for collecting demos in the wild, trained with Diffusion Policy.
  • Octo (2024): an open generalist robot policy using a diffusion action head.
  • π0 (Physical Intelligence, 2024): a vision-language-action model that generates action chunks with flow matching, a close cousin of diffusion.

Chapter 15

Recap & Quiz

Eight-line recap

  1. Behavior cloning copies human demos, but humans solve tasks in more than one way.
  2. Averaging those ways crashes; picking a way each instant jitters.
  3. Diffusion Policy generates actions the way DDPM generates images: start from noise, denoise step by step.
  4. It denoises a whole chunk of 16 future actions, conditioned on the latest 2 observations.
  5. It executes 8, then re-plans. This is receding-horizon control: smooth and responsive.
  6. Learning the score (gradient) avoids the normalising constant Z, so training is stable.
  7. Visual encoder trained end-to-end; DDIM gives ~0.1 s plans; position control works best.
  8. Result: +46.9% on average across 15 tasks, and strong real-robot skills from pushing to shirt folding.

Check yourself

What exactly is being “diffused” in Diffusion Policy?
A sequence of future robot actions At (e.g. 16 steps of end-effector poses and gripper widths), not images. Observations are used as conditioning, not generated.
Why does an averaging (regression) policy fail on multimodal demos?
Squared-error training learns the mean of the demonstrated actions. If half go left and half go right, the mean goes straight, often into the obstacle, an action no human ever took.
What do To, Tp and Ta stand for, and their typical values?
Observation horizon (2), prediction horizon (16) and action execution horizon (8). The robot plans 16, executes 8, then re-plans.
Why is training more stable than IBC?
IBC must estimate the intractable normaliser Z with negative samples. Diffusion Policy learns the score ∇a log p(a|o), in which ∇a log Z vanishes because Z doesn’t depend on the action, so Z never needs estimating.
Why condition on observations instead of generating them jointly?
The encoder runs once per decision instead of once per denoising step, making inference fast enough for real-time control and making end-to-end vision training practical.
Which action space worked best, and why is that surprising?
Position control. Most prior BC work used velocity control, and baselines got worse with position control; Diffusion Policy handles position control’s sharper multimodality and benefits from its lower compounding error.
CNN or transformer backbone: which should you try first?
The CNN (with FiLM conditioning): it works out of the box. Use the transformer for complex tasks with rapid action changes, expecting more tuning.

Glossary

Behavior cloning (BC)
Supervised learning of a policy from demonstration (observation, action) pairs.
Visuomotor policy
A policy mapping camera images (plus robot state) directly to motor commands.
Multimodal distribution
A distribution with several separate peaks, i.e. several distinct correct answers.
Explicit / implicit policy
Outputs actions directly / scores actions with an energy function and searches for the best.
Energy-based model (EBM), Z
Assigns low energy to likely inputs; Z is the normaliser summing over all inputs, usually intractable.
Score function
∇ log p: an arrow field pointing toward more probable actions.
Langevin dynamics
Sampling by following the score with small random kicks.
Receding-horizon control
Plan ahead, execute only the first part, re-plan with new information.
FiLM
Conditioning by letting an input scale and shift each feature channel of a network.
DDIM
A faster sampler that uses fewer denoising steps than the model was trained with.
Proprioception
The robot’s sense of its own joint/gripper positions.
Teleoperation
A human remotely driving the robot to record demonstrations.
