How would you build a segmentation pipeline for crop health monitoring, using camera imagery and the latest models?

The sensor and the labeling strategy decide more than the model architecture does. Covers both branches: multispectral fusion with SAM2/DINOv2-assisted labeling, and the RGB-only path when there's no NIR sensor at all.

1.1Start From the Sensor, Not the Model

A gimbal-mounted RGB camera alone can classify visible necrosis or wilting, but most stress signals — early water deficit, nutrient deficiency — show up in the near-infrared before they're visible to the eye. Fly RGB plus a modified NIR or dedicated multispectral sensor at a controlled altitude and ground-sample distance, then compute vegetation indices (NDVI, NDRE) alongside the RGB channels as additional input planes rather than as a separate downstream product.

1.2The Pipeline, Stage by Stage

For labeling, don't hand-annotate pixel masks from scratch. SAM2 gives strong zero-shot object masks from a point or box prompt, and running it over every tile turns "draw a mask" into "confirm or correct a proposed mask" — a large annotation-cost cut, especially for the long tail of disease presentations a fixed label set won't cover well. Vegetation indices double as a second, free source of weak labels for stress regions, bootstrapping the first training round before any human labeling happens at all.

1.3Choosing a Model

The trade-off is label efficiency vs. deployability, and the choice comes down to which one is actually scarce:

In practice this isn't either/or: SAM2 and DINOv2 do the heavy lifting during dataset construction and early-pilot iteration, and the model that actually ships to the field is the lightest one that hits the accuracy bar — usually a DeepLabV3+ or U-Net variant with an efficient backbone, distilled or fine-tuned from the foundation-model-labeled dataset, because that's the one that fits the edge power and thermal budget for in-field scouting rather than requiring a batch upload for offline processing.

Model architecture is almost never the bottleneck here. Field lighting, growth-stage variation, and occlusion from overlapping canopy dominate error far more than swapping Mask2Former for DeepLabV3+ — spend the marginal engineering hour on dataset diversity and domain-shift testing (different fields, different weeks, different weather) before spending it on model architecture search.

1.4Turning a Mask Into a Grower-Facing Answer

After inference, per-plant or per-row aggregation matters more than raw pixel accuracy for the output that actually gets used: connected-component analysis over the mask, reprojected onto the field ortho-mosaic with GPS metadata, turns a pixel mask into an actionable map — "these three rows are stressed" — rather than a segmentation image nobody in the field will look at directly.

Hands-on depth here is strongest at the edges of this pipeline — multi-camera capture, streaming, edge deployment on Jetson-class hardware — rather than having personally fine-tuned SAM2 or DINOv2 on crop imagery. Say that plainly if asked directly, then pivot to the deployment-constraint reasoning, which is real, shipped experience.

1.5The RGB-Only Branch: No Multispectral Sensor at All

Most real deployments — a phone in a grower's hand, a fixed-wing without a NIR payload — only have a standard RGB camera. You lose NDVI/NDRE's pre-visible stress signal entirely, but a full pipeline is still viable once it's built specifically around three channels instead of assuming a fourth.

Camera-specific preprocessing

Field lighting, not resolution, is what breaks an RGB-only pipeline first. Convert RGB to HSV or Lab before segmenting — HSV isolates brightness into its own channel (V), so shadow and harsh-sun variation stop leaking into the hue/saturation signal the vegetation mask actually depends on. Apply CLAHE (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization) to recover detail in overexposed leaf faces and under-canopy shadow without the blown-out look global histogram equalization gives. Pull focal length, aperture and sensor size from EXIF metadata to back out a millimeter-per-pixel scale at the known capture distance — without it, "lesion covers 40 pixels" has no physical meaning across photos taken from different heights or different phones.

Loss function matters more than usual because the target class is small — a rust fleck can be a few dozen pixels on a leaf that's thousands. Combine Dice Loss (rewards mask overlap directly, robust to class imbalance) with Focal Loss (down-weights easy, already-confident background pixels so gradient stays on the hard lesion boundary). Plain cross-entropy on this class ratio mostly learns to predict "healthy" everywhere and still scores deceptively well on pixel accuracy.

Severity, correctly oriented

severity_% = (disease_mask_pixel_area / total_leaf_mask_pixel_area) × 100

Disease area over total leaf area — not the other way around. Inverting the ratio is an easy mistake to paste into a doc and a bad one to ship: it turns a 4% infection into a reported 2500%.

Lesion counting via connected-component labeling on the disease mask is a second, independent signal: five small, separate lesions can mean an early, spreading infection worth flagging urgently even at a severity percentage that looks mild — severity percentage and lesion count diverge in a way that matters agronomically.

Field noise, RGB-only indices, and quantization

Leaves move in wind, lighting shifts constantly, phones auto-adjust exposure mid-shot. Train with heavy color jitter, synthetic glare/flare, and background substitution (swap the soil/weed background behind the leaf mask) so the model doesn't quietly learn "this particular soil color = healthy." Even without NIR, ExG = 2G − R − B (Excess Green) is a cheap, RGB-only vegetation index — feed it in as an explicit fourth input channel alongside R/G/B rather than relying on the network to rediscover the same relationship from raw color. For offline field use, export to ONNX / CoreML / TFLite with FP16 or INT8 quantization — same edge-deployment reasoning as the Jetson/RB5 constraints elsewhere in this book: target hardware's power and thermal budget decides precision, not model accuracy alone.

Same lesson as the multispectral branch above: the backbone swap between SegFormer and DeepLabV3+ buys less than getting the field-noise augmentation and the severity-ratio direction right. A correct model with an inverted metric is worse than a slightly weaker model with a correct one — it fails silently, in the grower's favor, right up until it doesn't.
Open leaf-disease datasets (PlantVillage, PlantDoc, PlantPathology) are mostly lab-shot: clean backgrounds, controlled lighting, single leaf centered in frame. A model trained only on these looks strong on validation and then degrades hard on real field photos with wind, shadow and clutter. Treat them as a pretraining source, not a finish line — budget for a field-shot fine-tuning set before trusting severity numbers on real deployments.
Where do you get open datasets for the RGB-only branch without hand-labeling from zero?

PlantVillage (leaf classification, some segmentation variants) and PlantDoc (real field-condition images, weaker labels) are the two most-cited starting points. PlantPathology 2020/2021 (Kaggle/FGVC) adds apple-specific foliar disease images with more label-noise realism. None are pixel-mask-labeled at scale out of the box — the practical move is the same SAM2 weak-label bootstrap from §1.2: run these datasets' bounding-box or classification labels through a promptable segmentation model to generate pixel masks, then human-correct a sample rather than hand-drawing from zero.

What does the PyTorch skeleton for the two-stage RGB pipeline actually look like?
import torch
from ultralytics import YOLO

leaf_model = YOLO("leaf_seg.pt")          # Stage A: plant/leaf mask
lesion_model = torch.jit.load("lesion_seg.pt").eval()  # Stage B: SegFormer/DeepLabV3+

def exg(img):  # img: float tensor, C,H,W in [0,1], channels R,G,B
    r, g, b = img[0], img[1], img[2]
    return (2 * g - r - b).clamp(-1, 1)

def score_leaf(rgb_frame):
    leaf_mask = leaf_model(rgb_frame)[0].masks.data[0]        # Stage A
    x0, y0, x1, y1 = bbox_from_mask(leaf_mask)
    roi = rgb_frame[:, y0:y1, x0:x1]
    roi_4ch = torch.cat([roi, exg(roi).unsqueeze(0)], dim=0)  # RGB + ExG

    with torch.no_grad():
        lesion_mask = lesion_model(roi_4ch.unsqueeze(0))[0].argmax(0)  # Stage B

    leaf_px = leaf_mask.sum().item()
    lesion_px = lesion_mask.sum().item()
    severity_pct = 100.0 * lesion_px / max(leaf_px, 1)
    lesion_count = count_connected_components(lesion_mask)
    return severity_pct, lesion_count

The shape that matters: Stage A crops before Stage B ever runs, ExG rides in as a fourth channel rather than a separate output, and the severity ratio is disease-over-leaf, matching the formula above.

How would you validate either branch before showing a grower real results?

Hold out a set of fields the model never saw during training or fine-tuning, and calibrate the model's health categories against an agronomist's ground-truth walk of those same rows — not against a second model's output. Track precision on the "stressed" class specifically, since a grower acts on false positives by wasting a field visit and on false negatives by missing real damage; the two failure modes aren't symmetric and the validation should say which one the current model leans toward.

How do you keep either model useful as disease presentations drift season to season?

Treat the SAM2-assisted labeling loop as a standing capability, not a one-time bootstrap: route the tiles the current model is least confident on back through the same weak-label-then-human-correct step each season, and retrain or fine-tune on the accumulated set. This is exactly where a foundation-model backbone pays for itself twice — once at initial dataset construction, and again every season as an active-learning source, instead of re-running a from-scratch labeling effort each time.
