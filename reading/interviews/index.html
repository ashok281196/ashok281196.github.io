Chapter I

SLAM & State Estimation

Cartographer, GPS-denied return-to-home, and vision-based state estimation under occlusion are classic SLAM interview territory. Your ArduPilot/EKF background is the natural point of comparison against research-depth answers on these topics.

1.1

Walk me through a SLAM pipeline like Cartographer — scan matching, submaps, loop closure.

rating — near-certain

Cartographer splits the problem into a local front-end and a global back-end. The front-end scan-matches each incoming LiDAR or point-cloud frame against the current local submap using a Ceres-based nonlinear least-squares optimizer, accumulating frames into that submap until it is "finished." The back-end treats every finished submap as a node in a pose graph, and periodically runs global optimization — including loop closure, where branch-and-bound scan matching checks whether the robot has revisited a previously mapped area and, if so, adds a constraint that snaps accumulated drift back toward consistency.

Frame your answer around the trade-off, not the diagram: smaller submaps close loops faster and bound local drift tighter, at the cost of more compute per unit distance and more graph nodes to optimize.

Marginalia
Expect a follow-up on tuning submap size for a fast aerial platform vs. a slow ground robot — faster platforms need smaller submaps, or higher-rate scan matching, to keep the front-end's local-linearity assumption valid between finalizations.
1.2

How would you handle GPS-denied return-to-home on a drone?

rating — near-certain

ArduPilot's EKF3, already running on my Strix H7 build, fuses IMU, compass and baro with GPS as one input among several — when GPS confidence drops it falls back on whatever else is available (optical flow, rangefinder), or degrades to IMU+baro dead-reckoning. That covers a few seconds of dropout, not a real GPS-denied RTH.

For that, I would add a VIO front-end — stereo or RealSense plus IMU, ORB-SLAM3-style — publishing pose corrections into the EKF at roughly 30Hz, and treat "return" as traversal of a recorded pose graph back through prior keyframes, rather than a straight flight to a remembered GPS fix.

This matters because relative transforms between keyframes compound error more gracefully than one absolute coordinate, and periodic loop closure against earlier keyframes corrects drift along the way — the return path gets more reliable the further you retrace it, instead of betting everything on a single final GPS fix.

1.3

EKF vs. UKF vs. factor-graph optimization — how do they differ, and why did ArduPilot pick EKF?

rating — likely

These three sit on a single spectrum of cost vs. accuracy vs. revisability. EKF linearizes the nonlinear process/measurement model around the current estimate via a first-order Taylor expansion — cheap, well understood, but once a state is finalized it can't be revisited, so an early bad association permanently biases everything downstream. UKF instead propagates a small set of sigma points through the true nonlinear function and reconstructs mean and covariance from their transformed spread, capturing second-order effects EKF throws away, at several times the compute cost per update. Factor-graph optimization — what actually underlies Cartographer's back-end, via Ceres — keeps a window of historical poses and re-solves the whole nonlinear least-squares problem whenever a new constraint arrives, at the highest cost but with far more resilience to bad associations via robust loss functions like Huber.

Table 1.1 — Choosing a state-estimation backbone
MethodCompute costCan revise past states?Typical use
EKFLowNoReal-time flight controller state (ArduPilot EKF3)
UKFMedium–highNoStrongly nonlinear systems where EKF linearization breaks down
Factor graphHighestYes (smoothing)Global SLAM back-end, loop closure (Cartographer, GTSAM/iSAM2)

ArduPilot uses EKF because flight-controller compute — an STM32 — is tight, and the attitude/position dynamics at typical update rates aren't nonlinear enough within a single step to justify UKF's cost. It's a compute-budget decision, not an accuracy ceiling.

1.4

How does vision-based state estimation degrade under occlusion or IR obstruction?

rating — likely

A pipeline that leans on a single vision or IR source treats occlusion as a total dropout rather than graceful degradation — the estimator has nothing left to fall back on the instant the marker or beacon is blocked. The fix is redundancy in the fusion hierarchy: IMU as the high-rate backbone driving prediction, vision or IR as a lower-rate correction layered on top, so a few hundred milliseconds of occlusion simply widens the covariance instead of losing the estimate outright. I would also gate incoming vision measurements with a Mahalanobis-distance test, so a corrupted or partially-occluded reading gets rejected rather than blindly fused in and dragging the estimate off course.

1.5

How do you fuse sensors at very different rates — IMU at 1kHz, camera at 30Hz, GPS at 5Hz?

rating — likely

This is exactly the pattern ArduPilot's EKF3 already implements, and it's what I would extend if I added a RealSense-based VIO correction to my own stack — the RealSense simply becomes another asynchronous update source slotted into the existing prediction/correction loop, not a redesign of the estimator itself.

Chapter II

Navigation Architecture

Expect at least one design-from-scratch whiteboard question here. Structure every answer in the same order — perception, localization, planning, control — and name the real system you've shipped at each layer rather than describing the abstraction alone.

2.1

Design a navigation stack from sensor input to actuator command.

rating — likely · whiteboard-style

Naming the real system you've shipped, layer by layer, reads far more senior than reciting the abstraction in a vacuum — it shows you've had to make the trade-offs at each boundary, not just memorized the diagram.

2.2

Architect a "return to origin if GPS is disrupted" system, as a whiteboard design prompt.

rating — likely

Continuously log a lightweight pose graph — keyframe plus relative transform to the last keyframe — while GPS is healthy, essentially as a free side effect of normal flight. The moment GPS is lost, switch the estimator to vision/IMU dead-reckoning and replan the return as a traversal of the last N keyframes, rather than a single absolute-position flight to a remembered GPS fix (see Fig. 1.2). Relative transforms compound error more gracefully than trusting one absolute coordinate, and periodic loop closure against earlier keyframes corrects drift along the way.

2.3

Global vs. local planners — what's the difference, and when do you re-plan?

rating — possible
Table 2.1 — Global vs. local planning
Global plannerLocal planner
InputFull known mapRolling window of live sensor data
AssumptionMap is staticHandles dynamic obstacles
FrequencyOn map or goal changeEvery control cycle (10–20 Hz)
Algorithm familyA*, D*DWA, TEB

Re-plan globally when the map itself changes meaningfully or the goal changes; re-plan locally every cycle regardless of whether anything changed — that's the loop actually keeping you safe in real time.

2.4

Explain ROS's costmap/nav2 concept and how you'd translate it into ga-msg.

rating — possible

Costmaps layer the static map, an inflation buffer, and dynamic obstacle detections into one cost surface the planner queries every cycle. In ga-msg I would replicate that layering as a shared-memory grid that perception nodes write into and the planner node reads from over our own pub/sub transport — the same conceptual decoupling ROS provides, without ROS's message-type and tf-tree machinery, which is precisely why ga-msg exists: ROS-like decoupling without the per-message serialization overhead that hurts on high-bandwidth hardware streams.

2.5

What is a costmap inflation layer, and why does it matter?

rating — possible

Without inflation, a planner could legally route the robot's center directly along an obstacle's edge — mathematically valid for a point, physically a collision for anything with actual width.

Chapter III

Firmware & RTOS

PX4/NuttX and ArduPilot/EtherCAT are the two flight-firmware architectures most likely to come up here. Your 200kHz control loop and the EndureAir failsafe story are your strongest material in the entire manual; they belong here.

3.1

NuttX vs. bare-metal vs. FreeRTOS — how do you choose for a flight controller?

rating — near-certain
Table 3.1 — Flight-controller software architectures
NuttX (PX4)ArduPilotBare-metal / FreeRTOS
ModelPOSIX-like RTOS, modular uORB pub/subTraditionally single-loop, modernized over timeNo OS abstraction, or a minimal scheduler
Dev speedFast — filesystem, drivers, threads providedModerate — mature ecosystem, longer flight historySlow — everything hand-built
DeterminismGood, not maximalGood, proven in the fieldHighest — no abstraction overhead
Best forCustom research flight stacksFlight-proven builds, broad hardware supportHard-real-time loops (my 200kHz EtherCAT/CAN loop)

The pattern to communicate: convenience and determinism sit on a spectrum, and you choose per subsystem, not per project. NuttX buys you speed of development on complex autopilot logic; bare-metal buys you a timing guarantee a heavier abstraction layer would put at risk.

Marginalia
Expect a follow-up of "why not NuttX for everything, then?" The honest answer is: I would, for anything that isn't sitting directly in a hard-real-time hot path.
3.2

Explain your 200kHz EtherCAT/CAN control loop — what makes that hard?

rating — near-certain · your strongest story

I kept the hot path entirely allocation-free and free of blocking calls, and used PI/PD control with a hybrid Kp-Kd term tuned per actuator so it converges without overshoot inside that budget. EtherCAT's distributed-clock synchronization was the other critical piece: it keeps every actuator phase-aligned to a shared time base rather than drifting relative to one another, which matters enormously once several actuators need to move in coordination.

3.3

What is priority inversion, and how do you prevent it?

rating — likely

Priority inheritance fixes this by temporarily boosting the low-priority mutex holder's priority to match the highest task blocked on it, so it finishes and releases the resource before a medium-priority task gets the chance to cut in. I would never let a shared mutex sit inside a 200kHz hot path at all — but for the medium-rate tasks around it, telemetry writeback, parameter updates, this is exactly the class of scheduling bug I'd check for with RTOS trace tooling before trusting timing under real load.

3.4

CAN bus arbitration vs. EtherCAT synchronization — how do they differ?

rating — likely
Table 3.2 — Fieldbus comparison
CANEtherCAT
TopologyMulti-master busMaster–slave, "processing on the fly"
ArbitrationBitwise non-destructive CSMA/CR by message-ID priorityFrame passes through every slave sequentially
Payload8 bytes (CAN 2.0), 64 bytes (CAN-FD)Effectively unbounded, Ethernet frame
Sync mechanismNone built-inDistributed-clock (DC), microsecond-level

Debugging low-level glitches on either bus starts at the physical layer — termination resistors, differential-signal integrity on a scope — before moving to a protocol analyzer to check error counters, and finally to firmware, watching for ring-buffer overrun or interrupt latency stalling the driver's read loop.

3.5

How did you bring up ArduPilot on custom hardware (Strix H7 via DFU)?

rating — likely

STM32CubeProgrammer over the DFU bootloader to flash the ArduPilot binary, then a staged bring-up: verify IMU, compass and baro all report sane values in the ground station before touching motors at all, calibrate ESC/RC endpoints, and confirm failsafe behavior — RC loss, GCS loss, battery failsafe — on the bench before ever arming outdoors. I treat failsafe validation as a hard gate, not an afterthought — a hard-won lesson, given the EndureAir loss described in Chapter IX.

3.6

Hard vs. soft real-time — where's the line in your stack?

rating — possible

Hard real-time: missing a deadline is a system failure — the actuator control loop, EtherCAT sync. Soft real-time: missing a deadline degrades quality without crashing anything — telemetry UI refresh, video bitrate adaptation. My GA-MSG middleware and GCS console target a 120ms latency budget rather than a hard deadline, while the actuator firmware is strictly hard real-time, and I design the two halves of the stack differently because of that distinction.

Chapter IV

Middleware & Multi-Agent Systems

Swarm-drone coordination meets your ga-msg design decisions directly. Expect a "why not just use ROS 2" challenge — answer it honestly, including what you give up.

4.1

Why ZeroMQ/gRPC over ROS 2 (DDS) for a custom middleware like ga-msg?

rating — near-certain
Table 4.1 — Middleware trade-offs
ROS 2 (DDS)ga-msg (ZeroMQ/gRPC)
EcosystemMature — tf, rviz, nav2Minimal, purpose-built
OverheadPer-message serialization + discovery, higher CPU / jitterLean transports: TCP/UDP + shared memory
Language spanGood, but heavier bindings per languageC++/Python bindings, gRPC gives strong typing across languages
Latency targetNot primarily latency-optimizedSub-120ms end-to-end, tuned specifically for hardware streams

The honest trade-off: ga-msg gives up ROS's tooling ecosystem entirely. That's acceptable because our fleet size and deployment shape don't need most of what that ecosystem provides — say this plainly rather than implying ga-msg is strictly better in every dimension, since it isn't.

4.2

How does heartbeat and link-loss failover work over a Doodle Labs mesh radio?

rating — near-certain

Heartbeats run at a fixed 1–5Hz, monitored by a sliding-window timer. On reaching the "link lost" state, an automated failsafe fires — RTH for a UAV, or a safe braking state for a ground unit. I built exactly this for the ga-mavlink bridge and field-tested it with deliberate Doodle Labs link-loss: the aircraft correctly triggered RTH on GCS disconnection.

4.3

How would you coordinate a swarm of agents sharing one middleware?

rating — likely

Each agent publishes its own state — pose, battery, task status — on a namespaced topic; the coordinator subscribes to every agent and performs task allocation and conflict resolution, publishing commands back down. Heartbeat-plus-timeout logic detects a dropped agent so the coordinator can reassign its task — the same pattern as the ga-msg-to-MAVLink bridge failover, just operating one level up, at the swarm layer.

4.4

How do you synchronize clocks across robots and ground stations on a mesh network?

rating — possible

Over wired Ethernet, PTP (IEEE 1588) gives sub-millisecond hardware-timestamped sync. Over a wireless mesh like Doodle Labs, PTP's assumption of symmetric, low-jitter links breaks down, so a more realistic approach is NTP/Chrony with round-trip filtering, or GPS PPS as a shared absolute reference when every node has GPS. For my own heartbeat/failsafe logic, consistent sequence-numbering and staleness detection matter more than microsecond precision — a cheaper problem, and the right one to actually solve.

Chapter V

Computer Vision

Ties crop-health segmentation pipelines to your own aerial-grasping patent work.

5.1

How would you build a segmentation pipeline for crop health monitoring?

rating — possible

Collect labeled field imagery spanning real lighting and growth-stage variation, train a semantic segmentation model — U-Net or DeepLab-style — to classify healthy, stressed and diseased regions per pixel, then aggregate per-plant statistics from the resulting mask. The hard part is almost never model architecture; it's dataset diversity — field lighting and occlusion variance dominates error far more than swapping one segmentation architecture for another.

5.2

Detection vs. segmentation vs. pose estimation — when would you use each on a drone?

rating — possible
Table 5.1 — Perception output types
OutputGives youGood for
DetectionBounding boxCoarse "is X present"
SegmentationPixel-level maskArea / coverage estimation (crop health %)
Pose estimationFull 6-DOF position + orientationPrecision tasks — landing on a marker, grasping

On my aerial-grasping patent work, pose estimation was non-negotiable — a bounding box alone tells you nothing about approach angle, and approach angle is exactly what determines whether a grasp succeeds.

5.3

Marker-based vs. markerless visual state estimation — what are the trade-offs?

rating — possible

Marker-based — AprilTag/ArUco — gives cheap, robust, low-latency pose estimates via a PnP solve against known 3D marker geometry, but requires instrumenting the environment ahead of time. Markerless monocular VIO resolves scale ambiguity by fusing IMU accelerometer integration over keyframe intervals inside a tightly-coupled nonlinear optimization or EKF, generalizes to unknown environments, but is more compute-heavy and can lose tracking entirely in low-texture scenes. I'd use markers for a controlled task like a precision landing pad, and markerless VIO for open-field GPS-denied navigation where the environment can't be instrumented in advance.

Chapter VI

Learning-Based Robotics

One-shot cobot learning and generative-AI applications — likely the least overlap with your resume. Keep these conceptually sharp rather than claiming hands-on depth you don't have.

6.1

What is one-shot / few-shot imitation learning, and how would you apply it to a cobot task?

rating — possible

This matters when collecting a large labeled dataset per task is infeasible on a real factory floor — exactly the constraint behind teaching an industrial cobot a new pick quickly, without re-engineering code per SKU.

6.2

Where do diffusion models fit into robotics beyond image generation?

rating — possible

Diffusion policies frame action generation as a denoising process conditioned on the current observation — useful precisely when the action distribution is multimodal, i.e. several genuinely different valid ways to grasp an object exist, and a single-mode regression policy would average across those modes into an action invalid for any of them. The trade-off is inference latency, which matters directly for anything sitting inside a real-time control loop.

6.3

How would you use a genetic algorithm in a robotics context?

rating — unlikely, but keep it ready

Good fit for offline parameter or controller optimization where the search space is large, non-differentiable, or riddled with local optima — evolving PID or controller gains against a simulated fitness function, then transferring the best candidates to real hardware. Not something to put inside a real-time loop; it's a tuning tool that runs before deployment, not during it.

Chapter VII

Video Streaming & Edge Deployment

Your own GStreamer/RTSP work meets edge AI accelerator deployment — a common thread in robotics interviews for any team shipping vision on embedded hardware.

7.1

How would you build adaptive-bitrate video over a lossy mesh link, driven by RSSI/packet loss?

rating — likely · matches your shipped work

This is close to what I actually shipped at GA: read link-quality metrics off the Doodle Labs radio at a few Hz, and drive the GStreamer hardware encoder's bitrate property down as those metrics degrade. On recovery from a bad loss burst, forcing an IDR/keyframe insertion avoids inheriting decode artifacts from an already-degraded stream. I stayed on RTP over UDP rather than TCP specifically because retransmission-on-loss causes head-of-line blocking — for a live operator feed, a dropped frame beats a frozen one waiting on a retransmit.

7.2

What actually adds latency in a camera-to-screen pipeline, and how do you strip it out?

rating — likely

The biggest wins are avoiding unnecessary memory copies — a hardware-accelerated zero-copy path (NVMM/DMABUF on Jetson-class hardware) straight from capture into the encoder — and disabling B-frames (tune=zerolatency), since B-frames need future frames buffered before they can even be encoded. RTP over UDP avoids head-of-line blocking on loss, and on the decode side a single-frame render buffer avoids the display pipeline queuing frames for smoothness at the direct cost of latency.

rating — likely

Chapter VIII

Manipulation & Kinematics

Bimanual arm work, pyroki, and FK/IK sit alongside classic cobot manipulation questions.

8.1

How do you solve forward and inverse kinematics for a 6-DOF arm in real time?

rating — possible

FK is a closed-form chain of transforms — Denavit-Hartenberg parameters or product-of-exponentials — no iteration needed. IK is the harder direction: for a clean geometric wrist configuration an analytical solver (IKFast-style) is fastest, but for anything without a closed form, or when smooth, singularity-aware motion is needed for teleoperation — which is what pyroki is doing under the hood — damped least-squares numerical IK with joint-limit constraints is more practical, even though it's iterative rather than instant.

8.2

How do you handle kinematic singularities during teleoperation?

rating — possible

Watch the Jacobian's conditioning — as its determinant approaches zero near a singularity, the standard pseudo-inverse produces unstable, oversized joint velocities for a tiny Cartesian motion. Damped least-squares adds a damping term to the inverse, J* = Jᵀ(JJᵀ + λ²I)⁻¹, so velocity stays bounded near the singularity, at the cost of some Cartesian tracking accuracy right at that moment — a trade worth taking every time over letting a joint velocity spike become a safety incident on a bimanual rig.

8.3

How do you implement force feedback in teleoperation, accounting for latency?

rating — possible

Torque or current sensing at the gripper gets low-pass filtered to strip noise, then mapped back to the operator's controller through an impedance or admittance control law. The part people miss: beyond roughly 20ms of round-trip latency, naive force reflection becomes unstable — it can genuinely start oscillating — so you need passivity-based control that dissipates the excess energy the delay introduces, rather than reflecting the force straight through as if the link were instantaneous.

Chapter IX

Systems & Leadership

Likely closing questions. Lean on your most concrete, differentiated stories rather than general principles — this is where the interview gets decided as much as in the technical chapters.

9.1

Design a fault-tolerant architecture for a drone operating in a swarm.

rating — possible

Isolate each layer as an independent process, with heartbeat monitoring across the boundaries — the same pattern from Chapter IV, applied at the architecture level. A hardware watchdog triggers a flight-controller-level fallback; a software watchdog triggers mission-abort or safe-landing if the onboard computer itself crashes.

9.2

How do you ensure zero-downtime, safe OTA updates for embedded robotics platforms?

rating — possible

Flash the new firmware to the dormant partition while the vehicle keeps running from the active one, then reboot into the new partition and run hardware self-tests, automatically reverting if boot fails or health checks don't clear within a timeout. This closely parallels the OTA architecture I designed for GA Console's AppImage fleet — per-device keys, a private update bucket, and electron-updater/PyInstaller bundling on the Steam Deck and Jetson targets.

9.3

You led a team through research → deployment → CI/CD — how do you structure that at GA?

rating — possible

I own HMI, middleware and firmware end to end at GA, and run a ga test framework — roughly 75 tests across Playwright E2E and FastAPI integration — so hardware-adjacent changes get validated before they ever reach a field test. That's the same research-to-CI/CD discipline that scales well across different toolchains and teams.

9.4

What's your approach to validating autonomy-stack changes before a live flight test?

rating — possible

Bench validation first — SITL or hardware-in-the-loop wherever possible — then a tiered field test: short-range low-risk maneuvers, extending range and complexity only once failsafes (RTH, link-loss, battery) are proven at that stage. That discipline would have caught the EndureAir link-dropout earlier, had link-loss been tested at that specific operating range beforehand rather than only on the bench.

9.5

Where does edge AI compute (Jetson/RB5/dedicated MLSoC) fit into your stack?

rating — possible

I've run multi-camera RealSense setups — D405/D435/D455 — avoiding DKMS/kernel driver issues on newer kernels, and deployed gRPC orchestration across Jetson, Steam Deck, and LattePanda nodes. My instinct is to reason about which compute node handles which workload based on power, thermal, and compute budget, rather than assuming any single box does everything — the same reasoning applies to slotting a dedicated inference accelerator into a specific workload rather than general-purpose compute.

9.6

Your own patent — autonomous aerial grasping. Walk me through the perception-to-actuation loop.

rating — possible

Target detection and pose estimation from the onboard camera, visual servoing to align the approach vector, a grasp trigger conditioned on distance and velocity thresholds, then gripper actuation with force feedback to confirm a secure grasp. The interesting failure modes lived almost entirely in approach-vector stability under wind disturbance — not in the grasp mechanism itself, worth saying explicitly since it shows where the real engineering difficulty was.

9.7

PX4 vs. ArduPilot — a direct architecture comparison.

rating — likely

PX4, built on NuttX, leans on a modular uORB internal pub/sub architecture and is generally seen as more research/dev-friendly for custom flight stacks. ArduPilot has a longer flight history and arguably more mature failsafe and mission-planning behavior, plus broader hobbyist and commercial hardware support — exactly why it was the choice for a from-scratch build like the Strix H7: the faster path to a flight-proven stack rather than a research platform.

Marginalia
Don't overclaim PX4/NuttX internals depth. Be direct that production depth is ArduPilot, then pivot to shared underlying concepts — EKF design, failsafe architecture — where both are equally strong ground.
9.8

Given ambiguous requirements — "make navigation more robust" — how do you scope the problem?

rating — possible

Ask what failure mode is actually costing them — GPS dropout, dynamic obstacles, mapping drift — because "robust navigation" looks completely different depending on which failure dominates in practice. Ship a fix for the top one or two real failure modes, the way RTH-on-link-loss was built, rather than chasing a generic "more robust" catch-all that doesn't target anything specific.

9.9

Tell me about a time you debugged a hard-to-reproduce field failure.

rating — near-certain · your best story

On a 1.2km waypoint mission the Doodle Labs link dropped at roughly 750m and the aircraft was lost. The post-mortem showed the RTH failsafe trigger threshold and the link's actual dropout behavior at that specific range had each been validated separately, but never together. The fix was testing failsafe triggers at the actual operating range rather than only on the bench, and folding link-loss testing into the standard pre-flight checklist permanently, not as a one-off.

Why this is your best story
It's concrete, it's yours, and it has a real failure with a real fix — exactly what a "tell me about a time" question is designed to surface. Have this one ready without hesitation.
