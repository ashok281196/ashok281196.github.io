I

Chapter one The problem, before any algorithm

You have a robot. It carries sensors, and every one of them lies a little. The wheel encoders slip, the IMU drifts, the GPS wanders by a few metres, the camera sees blurry corners. You also have a model of how the robot moves, and that model lies too, because you never know the wind or the friction exactly.

The question is not "which sensor is right." None of them is. The question is: given everything I have measured up to now, what is the probability distribution over where I actually am? Every method in this book is an answer to that one question. They differ only in what they are willing to approximate in order to compute the answer fast enough.

Notation Subscript k is the discrete time step. A hat means "our estimate of". A superscript minus means "before the measurement was folded in".

1.1 · The state, and why it is enough

The state \(x_k \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the smallest vector that captures everything about the system's past that matters for its future. For a ground robot that might be \([\,p_x, p_y, \theta\,]^\top\). For a drone with an IMU it is more like position, velocity, attitude quaternion, and the three-axis gyroscope and accelerometer biases — fifteen or sixteen numbers.

We assume the state evolves as a Markov process: the next state depends only on the current one, not the whole history. And we assume each measurement depends only on the state at the instant it was taken. Those two assumptions are what make everything tractable.

\[ x_k = f(x_{k-1},\, u_k) + w_k, \qquad w_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_k) \]
(1.1)
\[ z_k = h(x_k) + v_k, \qquad v_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_k) \]
(1.2)

Equation (1.1) is the process model — physics, kinematics, whatever you know about how the thing moves, driven by a control input \(u_k\). Equation (1.2) is the measurement model — what a perfect sensor would read if the state were \(x_k\). The noises \(w_k\) and \(v_k\) are what you admit you do not know, and their covariances \(Q_k\) and \(R_k\) are how loudly you admit it.

measurement at step k, dimension m
Pk
state covariance — how uncertain we are, and in which directions
Q, R

Jacobians of f and h

1.2 · The recursive Bayes filter

Read (1.3) as "the total probability of landing at xk, summed over every place we could have come from."

What we actually want is the belief: the posterior distribution \(p(x_k \mid z_{1:k})\). It can be computed recursively in two half-steps. First predict, which pushes the old belief forward through the motion model and always makes us less certain:

\[ p(x_k \mid z_{1:k-1}) = \int p(x_k \mid x_{k-1})\; p(x_{k-1} \mid z_{1:k-1})\; dx_{k-1} \]
(1.3)

Then update, which multiplies in the new evidence and always makes us more certain:

\[ p(x_k \mid z_{1:k}) \;\propto\; \underbrace{p(z_k \mid x_k)}_{\text{likelihood}} \; \underbrace{p(x_k \mid z_{1:k-1})}_{\text{prior}} \]
(1.4)

That is the whole theory. Everything that follows is engineering, because that integral is intractable for a general distribution. There are exactly two escape routes, and they define the two families in this book.

Route one: assume the belief is a Gaussian. Then you never store a distribution, only a mean \(\hat{x}\) and a covariance \(P\), and (1.3) and (1.4) collapse into matrix algebra. This gives you the Kalman filter, and its nonlinear cousins the EKF and UKF. Route two: stop tracking a belief at all. Instead keep every measurement ever taken and, whenever you need an answer, solve for the whole trajectory that best explains all of them at once. This gives you the factor graph.

Chapter two The Kalman filter: the exact case

Suppose the world is kind. Suppose \(f\) and \(h\) are linear, and every noise is Gaussian. Then something remarkable happens: a Gaussian pushed through a linear map is still exactly Gaussian, and the product of two Gaussians is exactly Gaussian. The belief never leaves the family, no approximation is ever made, and the Kalman filter is not a heuristic — it is the exact optimal solution.

You must understand this chapter before the next two, because the EKF and the UKF are both nothing more than "the Kalman filter, but with a trick for handling the curve."

In the linear case the models become matrices:

\[ x_k = F x_{k-1} + B u_k + w_k, \qquad z_k = H x_k + v_k \]
(2.1)

2.1 · Predict

Intuition Moving blind always widens the ellipse. That is the + Q.

Push the mean through the model, and push the covariance through it twice — once on each side, because covariance is quadratic in the state:

\[ \hat{x}_k^- = F\hat{x}_{k-1} + Bu_k \]
(2.2)
\[ P_k^- = F P_{k-1} F^\top + Q \]
(2.3)

2.2 · Update

Now a measurement arrives. Compute what you expected to see, \(H\hat{x}_k^-\), and subtract it from what you did see. The difference is the innovation, and it is the only new information in the world:

\[ y_k = z_k - H\hat{x}_k^-, \qquad S_k = H P_k^- H^\top + R \]
(2.4)
The whole filter in one line New estimate = old estimate + gain × surprise.

\(S_k\) is the innovation covariance: how surprised you were entitled to be. The Kalman gain then decides how much of that surprise to believe:

\[ K_k = P_k^- H^\top S_k^{-1} \]
(2.5)
\[ \hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_k^- + K_k y_k, \qquad P_k = (I - K_k H)\,P_k^- \]
(2.6)

Look at (2.5) and read it as a ratio. If \(P^-\) is large (you are lost) and \(R\) is small (the sensor is crisp), \(K\) tends toward \(H^{-1}\) and you snap onto the measurement. If \(P^-\) is tiny and \(R\) is huge, \(K \to 0\) and you ignore the sensor entirely. The gain is an automatic, optimal trust dial, and nobody has to tune it — it falls out of the covariances you declared.

Why this matters for the rest of the book The Kalman filter is exact only because linearity preserves Gaussianity. The moment \(f\) or \(h\) curves, a Gaussian pushed through it comes out lopsided and non-Gaussian, and the whole scheme becomes an approximation. The EKF and the UKF are two different ways of forcing the answer back into a Gaussian.

Chapter three The EKF: pretend it is a straight line

Reality curves. Rotations compose multiplicatively, a camera projects by dividing by depth, range to a beacon is a square root of a sum of squares. None of these is linear, so the Kalman filter's guarantee evaporates. The extended Kalman filter's answer is the most obvious one an engineer could give: if the function is curved, replace it with its tangent and carry on as though nothing happened.

3.1 · The first-order Taylor expansion

Expand \(f\) about the current estimate \(\hat{x}_{k-1}\) and throw away everything past the linear term:

\[ f(x) \;\approx\; f(\hat{x}_{k-1}) + F_k\,(x - \hat{x}_{k-1}), \qquad F_k = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat{x}_{k-1},\,u_k} \]
(3.1)
Jacobian The n×n matrix of every partial derivative of every output with respect to every input. It is the best linear approximation of f at one specific point.

Likewise \(H_k = \partial h / \partial x\) evaluated at the predicted state \(\hat{x}_k^-\). Now substitute those Jacobians into the Kalman equations wherever \(F\) and \(H\) appeared. One subtlety matters enormously and is the source of most EKF bugs:

\[ \underbrace{\hat{x}_k^- = f(\hat{x}_{k-1}, u_k)}_{\text{use the true nonlinear } f}, \qquad \underbrace{P_k^- = F_k P_{k-1} F_k^\top + Q}_{\text{use the Jacobian}} \]
(3.2)

The mean travels through the real function; only the covariance is transported by the linear approximation. The update step follows the same pattern:

\[ y_k = z_k - h(\hat{x}_k^-), \qquad K_k = P_k^- H_k^\top \left( H_k P_k^- H_k^\top + R \right)^{-1} \]
(3.3)

3.2 · What goes wrong

You linearise about a guess. If \(\hat{x}\) is off, the Jacobian is evaluated at the wrong point, the covariance update is wrong, the gain is wrong, and the next estimate is worse. The error compounds. This is divergence, and it is the classic EKF failure.

The covariance becomes a lie. Truncating the Taylor series discards real uncertainty, so \(P\) reports more confidence than is warranted. An overconfident filter down-weights good measurements, which makes it more wrong, which makes it more overconfident.

You must supply Jacobians. By hand, for a sixteen-dimensional state, with quaternions in it. They are tedious, error-prone, and silently wrong when you get a sign backwards.

Still everywhere ArduPilot, PX4 and almost every flight controller run an EKF. It is O(n3) at worst, needs no iteration, and hits a hard 400 Hz deadline on a microcontroller. Correctness is not the only axis.

And yet the EKF remains the workhorse of embedded state estimation, because it is cheap, deterministic, single-pass, and — when the sampling rate is high enough that the state barely moves between steps — the linearisation error is genuinely small.

Chapter four The UKF: do not differentiate, sample

Julier and Uhlmann began from an observation that sounds almost too simple: it is easier to approximate a probability distribution than to approximate an arbitrary nonlinear function. So do not linearise the function at all. Instead choose a small, deliberate set of points that carry the mean and covariance exactly, push each one through the real nonlinear function, and fit a Gaussian to wherever they land.

This is the unscented transform, and the UKF is what you get when you use it in place of the Jacobians in Chapter III.

4.1 · Choosing the sigma points

For an \(n\)-dimensional state you need \(2n+1\) points. One sits at the mean; the rest are placed symmetrically along the principal axes of the covariance, at a distance set by a scaling parameter:

\[ \mathcal{X}^{(0)} = \hat{x}, \qquad \mathcal{X}^{(i)} = \hat{x} \pm \left( \sqrt{(n+\lambda)P} \right)_i, \quad i = 1 \dots n \]
(4.1)
Matrix square root Take the Cholesky factor L with LL⊤ = P. Column i of L is the i-th axis of the uncertainty ellipsoid.

where \(\lambda = \alpha^2(n+\kappa) - n\) controls the spread. Typical settings are \(\alpha \approx 10^{-3}\), \(\kappa = 0\), and \(\beta = 2\) which is optimal when the true distribution really is Gaussian. Each point carries two weights, one for reconstructing the mean and one for the covariance:

\[ W_m^{(0)} = \frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda}, \quad W_c^{(0)} = \frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda} + (1 - \alpha^2 + \beta), \quad W_m^{(i)} = W_c^{(i)} = \frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)} \]
(4.2)

4.2 · Propagate and recombine

Push every point through the true function — no derivatives anywhere — then take the weighted mean and weighted scatter of the results:

\[ \mathcal{Y}^{(i)} = f\!\left(\mathcal{X}^{(i)}\right), \qquad \hat{x}^- = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_m^{(i)} \mathcal{Y}^{(i)} \]
(4.3)
\[ P^- = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_c^{(i)} \left( \mathcal{Y}^{(i)} - \hat{x}^- \right)\left( \mathcal{Y}^{(i)} - \hat{x}^- \right)^\top + Q \]
(4.4)

The measurement step is identical in spirit. Push the sigma points through \(h\), get \(\hat{z}^-\) and the innovation covariance \(P_{zz}\), and additionally form the cross-covariance between state and measurement:

\[ P_{xz} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} W_c^{(i)} \left( \mathcal{X}^{(i)} - \hat{x}^- \right)\left( \mathcal{Z}^{(i)} - \hat{z}^- \right)^\top, \qquad K = P_{xz}\,P_{zz}^{-1} \]
(4.5)

Compare (4.5) with the EKF's \(K = P^-H^\top S^{-1}\). They are the same formula: \(P^-H^\top\) is the linear estimate of the cross-covariance. The UKF simply computes it by sampling instead of by differentiating.

Accuracy claim For a Gaussian input the unscented transform captures the true posterior mean and covariance correctly to third order in the Taylor expansion, whereas the EKF is correct only to first order in the mean and second order in the covariance. It buys this for roughly \(2n+1\) evaluations of \(f\) instead of one, and no Jacobians at all.

The catch is that the UKF is still a filter. It still assumes the posterior is Gaussian, it still processes measurements once and forgets them, and it still cannot go back and revise a state it committed to ten seconds ago. For that you need to abandon filtering altogether.

Chapter five Factor graphs: keep everything, solve it all

A filter is a machine that forgets. It compresses the entire past into one mean and one covariance, and once that compression has happened the information is gone for good. The factor graph refuses to compress. It writes down every unknown and every measurement as a graph, and treats estimation as one large optimisation problem rather than a recursion.

5.1 · The graph

A factor graph is a bipartite graph with two kinds of node. Variable nodes are the things you do not know: robot poses at each time, IMU biases, landmark positions, camera calibration. Factor nodes are the things you measured, and each one connects only to the variables it actually constrains.

Why bipartite A GPS factor touches one pose. An odometry or preintegrated-IMU factor touches two consecutive poses. A visual observation touches one pose and one landmark. Almost nothing touches everything — that is the sparsity the solver exploits.

The joint posterior over the whole trajectory \(X = \{x_0 \dots x_K\}\) factorises exactly along the graph:

\[ p(X \mid Z) \;\propto\; \prod_{i} \phi_i\!\left( X_i \right) \]
(5.1)

where \(X_i\) is the small subset of variables that factor \(i\) touches. Assume each factor is Gaussian in its residual, take the negative logarithm, and the product becomes a sum. Maximising the posterior turns into minimising a sum of squares:

\[ X^\star = \arg\min_{X} \sum_i \left\lVert h_i(X_i) - z_i \right\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i} \]
(5.2)

The notation \(\lVert e \rVert^2_{\Sigma} = e^\top \Sigma^{-1} e\) is the Mahalanobis norm — squared error, weighted by how much you trust that sensor. This single equation is the whole of modern SLAM, bundle adjustment and visual-inertial odometry.

5.2 · Solving it

Equation (5.2) is a nonlinear least-squares problem, so solve it the standard way. Linearise every residual about the current estimate, stack them into one tall sparse matrix \(A\) and one vector \(b\), and solve for a correction \(\delta\):

\[ \delta^\star = \arg\min_{\delta} \lVert A\delta - b \rVert^2 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \left(A^\top A\right)\delta^\star = A^\top b \]
(5.3)
\[ X \leftarrow X \oplus \delta^\star, \qquad \text{repeat until } \lVert\delta^\star\rVert < \varepsilon \]
(5.4)
The key word is repeat The EKF linearises once, at a guess it can never revisit. Gauss–Newton linearises again and again, each time at a better point. That difference alone removes the EKF's main failure mode.

That is Gauss–Newton; adding a damping term \((A^\top A + \mu I)\) gives Levenberg–Marquardt, which is what you actually use in practice. \(A^\top A\) is the information matrix, and it is extremely sparse because each factor touches only a couple of variables. Sparse Cholesky or QR factorisation exploits that, which is the only reason solving for ten thousand poses at once is feasible at all.

5.3 · Paying for the memory

Keeping everything cannot be free, and three techniques make it practical.

Marginalisation. Drop old variables but keep their information as a dense prior on whatever they touched, via the Schur complement. Marginalise everything except the newest pose and you have — precisely — a filter.

Fixed-lag smoothing. Keep a sliding window of the last few seconds, marginalise anything older. This is the pragmatic middle ground, and what most production visual-inertial systems actually run.

Incremental solving. iSAM2 and the Bayes tree notice that a new measurement disturbs only a small part of the graph, and re-factorise only that part. You get near-batch accuracy at near-filter cost, which is why GTSAM-based systems can run in real time.

6.1 · The unifying statement

Proposition An extended Kalman filter is a factor graph in which every variable except the newest one has been marginalised out, and in which each factor is linearised exactly once, at the estimate available when it first arrived.
Worth memorising Marginalise the past → you get a filter. Keep the past and re-linearise → you get a smoother. Everything else is implementation.

Once you see this, the whole taxonomy collapses into a single axis. Filtering and smoothing are not rival theories; they are the two ends of a spectrum, and fixed-lag smoothing is the dial in between. The UKF sits off to one side of that axis entirely — it is a filter, at the same point on the spectrum as the EKF, differing only in how it moves a Gaussian through a curve.

6.2 · How to choose

Reach for an EKF when you have a hard real-time deadline on constrained hardware, a high update rate that keeps the linearisation error small, and no need to ever revisit the past. Attitude and position estimation on a flight controller is the archetype.

Reach for a UKF when the nonlinearity is strong enough that a tangent misleads you, or when \(f\) is a lookup table, a simulation, or something else you cannot differentiate cleanly. It is a drop-in replacement with the same interface and a modest constant-factor cost.

Reach for a factor graph whenever the same place can be observed twice. Loop closures, multi-sensor calibration, offline trajectory refinement, anything where a measurement arriving now should be allowed to change your opinion about an hour ago. This is why every serious SLAM and visual-inertial system built in the last decade is a graph optimiser and not a filter.

In a real robot these coexist rather than compete. The autopilot runs an EKF at hundreds of hertz because it must. The companion computer runs a factor graph at a lazier rate because it can afford to think. Choosing between them is usually not a question of which is better, but of which layer of the stack you are standing in.
